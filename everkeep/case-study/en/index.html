Case study · Everkeep

Keeping a child's fragile crafts as a world that grows

The clay, pottery, New Year couplets and paper turkeys my child made between ages 4 and 6 crack, fade and get taken apart. I rebuilt them from photos as 3D pieces that turn and move, and gave them a small island and a gallery: the child plays on the island, the family keeps and shares them in the gallery. The first version serves one family. Its job is to set the quality bar and the experience before deciding how every child could upload their own work.

Opportunity

Crafts break, and photos are too flat

Every home has a corner piled with a child's work. Parents usually have three options, and each one loses something.

What parents do today

  • Take a photo: the look is kept, but it can't be turned, and it's lost in the camera roll within months
  • Keep the object: it takes space, clay cracks, paper fades, pieces get taken apart and remade
  • Throw it away: the easiest option, and the hardest to do

Three things a photo leaves out

  • Depth: these are things a child holds and turns in their hands
  • Life: to a child, the hen delivers food and the bear carries pancakes
  • Time: you only see a child grow when the work from age 4 to 6 sits side by side

Why now

  • A phone can shoot photos from many angles and a short video circling the piece
  • Apple Object Capture rebuilds 3D from photos on an ordinary Mac
  • iPhone and Android browsers open AR directly, and web 3D runs well on phones
Users

One product, three kinds of people

Each of them moves at a completely different pace. The product's structure grew out of that difference.

The child, age 6

Wants to turn something, move it around, take a look, but won't play for long and can't really read yet. Needs big buttons, dragging, and a response to every tap.

Parents

Want to keep, organize and share the work, and to decide when the child gets to play. Adding a new piece shouldn't take extra effort.

Family and friends who get the link

No download, no sign-in; it opens on a phone. What they see is a numbered piece with an age and the original photo, not a game.

Strategy

Get one family right first, then think about every child

The end goal is that any child can upload photos and grow a world of their own. But before automating anything, you need to know what "good" looks like. I split the road into three stages. Each stage answers one question, and its answer becomes the spec for the next.

That's why the brand is Everkeep and not "something island": the island is only the first region. Later worlds can grow new places from the kinds of work that come in.

Principles

Six rules every trade-off is checked against

Build what's there, don't fill in for the child

Every part is measured against the front photo for size, position and outline. If the left and right sides differ, the model differs too. The child has to recognize it as theirs at a glance; the whole product's trust starts there.

Repair damage, keep character

Cracks, mold and broken joints are left out. Hand-pressed bumps, fingerprints and uneven pools of glaze stay. True to the original, lightly polished.

The work is alive

Each piece has a move of its own: the stegosaurus walks in circles, the turkey fans its tail, a character on a New Year couplet turns into a hen and goes off to deliver food. Even standing still, they breathe a little.

Play for the child, a collection for the adults

The two jobs get two views. The island is for messing around; the gallery is quiet, with labels, and shared links open there.

Usable without reading

Big buttons, icons, a response to every tap. Details live in a folded label that opens on tap, but the main action, "View in 3D", is always showing.

Open and look, then take it with you

No sign-in; it can go on the home screen and opens offline. AR puts the piece on the table at real size, and the 3D-print file is ready to print.

Experience architecture

From one real object to a world that's a little different every day

The product's backbone is a single line: a real object comes in, becomes 3D you can turn, splits into two views, and finally comes back to the table at real size.

Decision log

Seven key trade-offs

Decision 01

What the home screen should be

Context
The child needs to play; the family needs to keep and share. The two run at completely different paces.
Options
A handmade island on the table A museum gallery A tiny planet
Call
Both the island and the gallery, as two views: the island for the child, the gallery for keeping. Tapping a piece on the island can take you straight to its plinth in the gallery. Shared links open in the gallery. The tiny planet stays as brand imagery.
Result
One product serves both paces without giving up either.
Decision 02

How to build a child's imagination

Context
At 4, my child wrote a New Year couplet, 事事如意 ("may all go as you wish"). The family thought the character 如 looked like a hen carrying a food-delivery box.
Call
The silhouette has to match the character exactly: flat on the couplet it is the character; press "Go out" and it puffs up, grows feathers and hops off the paper. Its legs are the character's own two strokes, with no real chicken legs added. The couplet hangs on the door of the island's log cabin.
Result
It became the most memorable moment in the product. The rule since: a child's imagination is the content itself, not raw material for us to reinterpret.
Decision 03

Can automatic photo rebuilds be used as-is

Context
It has to be automated eventually. I tried the off-the-shelf Apple Object Capture on four pieces, using the photos we already had.
Test
The taxi: 21 photos, a shape in 37 seconds. The stegosaurus and the bird had only been shot from one side and came out in fragments. The turkey is taped to a window, and the whole window was treated as one object.
Call
Use rebuilds only as a reference for shape and texture, and repair damage by the principles. At the same time, write a photo guide: piece on a table, one circle at eye level and one slightly above.
Result
Automation is held back mainly by the input, not the algorithm. Capture guidance will be the first screen of the future product.
Decision 04

Realism versus playfulness

Context
After the island got a real sky, a filmic tone curve and trees built leaf by leaf, the detail improved a lot, but the contrast was too strong and the whole scene ran dark.
Call
Keep the detail; raise the exposure, add more sky light, soften the shadows; nights get a bluish moonlight instead of going black. From then on, every lighting change is reviewed as three columns side by side: original, current, this change.
Result
A rule for every product: realistic doesn't mean dark. A product for children should still be easy to see at night.
Decision 05

Where the buttons go

Context
In full-screen 3D, more buttons means more of the work is covered, and it's worse on a phone.
Options
Three layouts, mocked up and compared side by side on phone and desktop.
Call
Only the brand and share at the top; all frequent controls in one bar at the bottom, within thumb reach. Select a piece and the bar springs into that piece's action bar. Everything is one size smaller on phones, giving the screen back to the work.
Result
Everything works one-handed, and the piece is never hidden behind a title or a button.
Decision 06

Parental control without accounts

Context
The parent's need was simple: sometimes the child needs to be kept off the island. But the product deliberately has no accounts.
Call
Hold the logo in the top-left corner for 2 seconds and answer a multiplication question to lock or unlock the island. When locked, only the gallery opens. The setting is stored on that device.
Result
No account, no password: multiplication the child can't do yet is the gate.
Decision 07

Should a tap leave something behind

Context
Tapping the grass used to grow a flower. But the flowers couldn't be removed, and after enough tapping the island became a mess.
Call
Separate feedback from lasting change: an ordinary tap sends out a soft ring of light and draws a butterfly over. To plant things, use "Decorate the island", where everything can be picked up, erased, or cleared at once.
Result
Every tap gets a response, and the world never gets messed up.
Quality bar

Turning "does it look right" into a standard you can measure

The most important output of the one-family version isn't seven pieces. It's a quality bar that can be handed over and later automated. The level of detail reached on the first piece, the stegosaurus, became the minimum for every piece after it, and the process became a written method each new piece follows.

  1. Understand the pieceZoom into every part: flat or round, how big relative to the body; tell the original apart from damage
  2. Calibrate the cameraRender the 3D with the phone's main-camera field of view, and align size and position on an easy-to-spot part
  3. Verify the referenceCheck whether the parts farthest from the reference line up; if not, fix the reference, not the parts
  4. Red and blue outlinesTrace the photo's outline in red and the 3D outline in blue; wherever they separate is what needs fixing
  5. Whole, then parts, then surfaceProportions and positions → edges and forks → color, bumps, gloss
  6. Change only what was pointed outBefore saying "fixed", prove it changed with measurements and traced outlines
Lesson

On the bird, the tilt of the comparison camera was set in the wrong direction, and I spent several rounds fixing wings against a reference that was off by 16 degrees, getting worse each time. Step 3 comes from that: after two or three rounds that don't line up, stop and check the method and the reference.

How I worked

I made sure we built the right thing; AI built it

The 3D rebuilds, shaders, interaction, AR files, deployment and the intro video were all implemented by an AI coding agent. My job was to set the direction and the quality bar, choose between options, and sign off on every result. These rules kept the work moving without drifting.

Working rules

  • Big directions start as mock-ups for me to pick from: 3 home screens, 3 button layouts, 10 site icons
  • Feedback is a circled or marked-up screenshot; change only what was pointed out, nothing else
  • "Fixed" needs evidence: overlays, measurements, phone screenshots
  • Feedback that would hold for any product goes into a shared style guide, so I never have to say it twice
  • Mistakes are reported plainly: what went wrong, for how long, and whether it's fixed

Calls I made

  • Serve one family first and define "good" at the highest standard, then think about scale
  • Treat a child's imagination as the content itself, with no reinterpretation from us
  • No accounts: shared links, a parental lock and on-device settings cover the need first
  • The intro video has to show the real interface in use, not just pretty shots
  • Realistic is fine, dark is not; a sense of life is fine, distraction is not
What's next

Next

  • Reshoot the stegosaurus, bird and turkey with the photo guide, run the automatic rebuild again, and measure how much manual repair each piece needs
  • Turn the red-and-blue outline comparison into a score and let the program tune parameters until they match: the first step toward automation
  • Decide how shapes get generated automatically (multiple photos, video, or 3D generated from images), and how good they must be to ship
  • Have a few families try shooting, and see how often ordinary parents get usable material with the guide
  • Before opening to other families, work out how children's photos and names are stored and shown
