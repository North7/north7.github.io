Case study · Peelback

Real vehicles, peeled open layer by layer for kids

Kids ride the bus, watch planes and wait for the bullet train every day, and the question they ask most is "what's inside?" Peelback is 3D on the web for an iPad or a phone: four vehicles built from real-world data that come apart from the shell all the way down to the engine. Every part has a name, reads itself aloud and comes with one number you can count. Every Chinese character carries zhuyin, so kids who can't read yet can still play on their own, and there is a full English mode.

Opportunity

Kids ask what's inside, and grown-ups often can't answer

Vehicles are things kids see and touch every day without ever seeing inside. Every tool a parent can reach for leaves something out.

What people use today

  • Picture books and cutaways: dozens of labels crammed on one flat page, and you can't turn it over
  • Videos: beautifully shot, but you can only watch; a kid can't point and ask
  • Toys and models: the ones that come apart don't look real, and the real-looking ones don't come apart

What kids actually want

  • Hands on: spin it, look underneath, take it apart
  • A little at a time: one layer, a few parts, then the next layer
  • That exact one: the bus they rode today, not "a bus"

Why now

  • iPad and phone browsers can run real-time 3D with soft shadows and lighting
  • Detailed models under open licences (engines, landing gear, train cars) can lend parts
  • An AI coding agent lets one person build the data, interaction and three languages for four vehicles
Users

The player can't read; the grown-up beside them has to get it right

Kids and adults look at the same screen for different things. The product only gets opened together if both are looked after.

Kids, 4 to 7

Mostly can't read yet. They spin, tap and pinch with a finger. Attention is short, so every touch needs a response, and the vehicle has to be the one they see every day: the orange roof of a Taipei bus, the orange stripe of the bullet train.

The grown-up beside them

Has to keep up with a chain of "why?" questions. Get something wrong and a kid remembers it for a long time, so the content has to be right. A number to talk about helps too, like "this battery could charge 5,000 phones".

Where it gets opened

At the bus stop, on the train, before bed. Often a phone held upright in one hand, so the buttons sit within thumb reach and long vehicles still need to read clearly on a tall, narrow screen.

Strategy

Start with the vehicles kids see every day, and take each one all the way

There are more vehicles than anyone could build. Instead of chasing numbers, I picked four with completely different shapes that kids in Taiwan actually live with: a box (the bus), a tube with wings (the plane), a free-form surface (the car) and a very long train. If the teardown framework holds up for all four, it works, and every vehicle after that is a matter of filling in data.

Principles

Six rules every trade-off is checked against

01One layer at a time

Layers go from outside in: outside, inside, chassis, engine. Only this layer's 4 to 9 parts come off and get labels; the others fade, and the camera frames this layer.

02Playable without reading

Every Chinese character on screen carries zhuyin, and tapping a name reads the description aloud. A three-way switch in the corner (ㄅㄆ｜中｜EN) changes the script, and English mode switches the voice as well.

03Everything has a source

Dimensions, doors and windows, seat layouts and part positions follow manufacturer specs, seat maps, rescue manuals and photos of the real vehicles. Where there was no data, it says "this part is inferred".

04Gestures first, buttons as shortcuts

One finger spins freely through 360°, including underneath; two fingers zoom. When the camera is moving on its own, one touch stops it. Every gesture also has a visible button.

05System buttons and vehicle buttons stay apart

The top-right corner holds only things unrelated to the content: language, sound, day and night. Everything that acts on the vehicle (take apart, X-ray, lights, drive) sits at the bottom, within thumb reach.

06Smooth from the first second

A vehicle downloads only when you open it. Slow devices start at conservative quality and effects fade in once the frame rate proves it can take them. No kid should stare at a frozen screen.

Decision log

Eight key trade-offs

Decision 01

What the product should look like

Context
The same teardown features could wear many faces. That decides whether a kid's first impression is a toy, a textbook or an exhibition.
Options
Three directions, each mocked up as desktop and phone home screens plus a vehicle view, side by side: A Blueprint B Peel orange C Gallery
Decision
C: one vehicle in the middle of the screen at a time, like walking up to a single exhibit. Its buttons, though, were separate circles where selection was only a thin ring, which wasn't clear enough. Related buttons now share one frosted capsule, and the selected one fills solid mint.
Result
One button language across the whole product. Outlines are kept for things you can't press yet, like "coming soon".
Decision 02

Taking it apart without the mess

Context
In the first bus, pressing "take apart" sent 29 parts flying with 29 labels at once. The screen was all name tags and kids didn't know where to look.
Options
Side parts list Dots, names on tap Names on zoom Thumbnail strip Layer by layer
Decision
Layer by layer, labelling only the current layer. Switching layers started as left and right arrows, which hid how many layers there were and made skipping impossible; it became a layer bar with an icon and a name per layer, all visible at once, so a tap jumps straight there.
Result
At most 9 names on screen. How deep you've peeled becomes progress in itself, and kids want to reach the last layer.
Decision 03

Where the zhuyin goes

Context
Most 4 to 7-year-olds read zhuyin before characters, so every Chinese character needs it, buttons and labels included. Buttons are small, and zhuyin crowds them fast.
Options
Three rounds: textbook-style vertical text (too cramped, looked messy) → horizontal above the characters (pushed line height up) → a smaller vertical column.
Decision
A vertical column to the right of each character, about 35% of its size, so three symbols stack to the character's height without stretching the line. The tone slot is always reserved so spacing stays even. Zhuyin is generated at build time: readings for characters with several pronunciations are chosen by word, then corrected with a table of Taiwanese readings.
Result
New text gets zhuyin on the next build with no manual tagging; the generated table gets a read-through after every copy change.
Decision 04

How real is real enough

Context
The first bus used estimated dimensions and a "generic low-floor bus" layout. It looked like a bus, just not the one kids ride every day.
Decision
Everything needs a source, and before building, a list of what follows real data and what is still inferred. The bus was rebuilt from the coachbuilder's published specs and photos and interior video of Taipei Bus vehicles; the plane follows Airbus's airport planning manual and the STARLUX seat map row by row; the bullet train's 977 seats follow the official seat maps car by car.
Trade-off
THSR doesn't publish where each car's underfloor equipment sits, so it follows the Japanese 700 Series it is based on, and the data notes say so.
Result
Grown-ups can explain from it with confidence. What's inferred is written down instead of quietly filled in with a generic layout.
Decision 05

Build the shapes, or borrow models

Context
A bus is a box and a fuselage is a tube; dimensions and parameters get them right. A car is a free-form surface everyone sees daily. Two centimetres off and it looks like another brand, and a hand-tuned Model Y looked wrong at a glance.
Decision
Decide per vehicle: borrow detailed parts where an openly licensed model exists (the plane's engines and landing gear, the train's cab and cars, the Model Y's body and chassis), then correct their positions against real dimensions and manuals; build the rest (the bus body in Blender from specs, the plane's mid-fuselage drawn in code from measured widths and heights).
Result
Every source is credited inside the product. The Model Y body is licensed for non-commercial use, so it has to be replaced before the product ever charges money.
Decision 06

Where buttons and panels go

Context
As features grew, three problems showed up. The lights button sat in the top-right corner next to language and day/night. On phones, the "what's different" card covered the layer bar and pushed into the driving numbers. And the day/night key controlled the page theme while the lights panel had its own day/night for the 3D, so the page could be at night while the car sat in daylight.
Decision
Two kinds of buttons: system functions top right, vehicle actions in the bottom bar (all on one row when they fit, a "more" menu only when they don't, and that menu stays open after a press). Floating panels stack from the bottom up, each sitting on the one below, with one card open at a time. Day and night became a single switch for page and vehicle together.
Result
With a phone upright in one hand, every vehicle action is within thumb reach and no panel covers another.
Decision 07

How fast it has to open

Context
All four vehicles' models and textures were bundled into the page: 10.5 MB, with all four decompressed on load and 343 MB of memory. On large desktop screens it also stuttered at first and flashed before turning smooth.
Decision
Models became separate files that download only when that vehicle opens, with content-hashed names cached for a year. Quality now starts conservative and climbs: ambient occlusion starts off and fades in only once the real frame time proves fast enough, and the canvas has a pixel cap.
Result
1.7 MB page, about 7 MB of memory on load; desktop frames at the start went from 110–150 ms to about 35 ms.
Decision 08

Helping first-time visitors get it

Context
You only discover that things come apart by trying. The first look at the gallery is just a vehicle, with no hint of how deep it goes.
Options
Five opening drafts built with the product's real 3D models: Particle morph Scan line Frozen explosion True scale Torn poster, plus a later Cinematic cut
Decision
The cinematic cut: one scene and one camera moving continuously. Particles form a Model Y, it peels apart layer by layer the same way it does in the product, the camera visits the seats and the battery, then all four line up at true scale before night falls and their lights come on one by one. Colours, buttons, day/night and all three languages come straight from the product.
Result
About 31 seconds to show what the product does, ending on "enter the gallery". It can be skipped and replayed from the gallery. The slowest frame is 76 ms (it used to freeze for 0.5 to 1 s).
Accuracy bar

Turning "looks real" into a list you can check

Things made for kids deserve more care, not less. Before each vehicle, I list what follows real data and what is inferred, chase data for the inferred parts, and write down whatever can't be found. Here is where it stands now.

Lesson

While building the intro film, I took colours from an old block at the top of the product's stylesheet and made several versions in blue. The colour actually in effect was the mint that a later block overrides it with. Now product colours are always read from what's live on screen, or the work happens inside the product itself, never from a hand-copied palette.

How I worked

I own "building the right thing"; AI does "building the thing"

Cleaning up 3D models, the teardown animation, the zhuyin system, lighting, the intro film and deployment were all implemented by an AI coding agent. I chose which vehicles to build, set the bar for accuracy and experience, decided between options and signed off every result.

Working rules

  • Big directions come as mock-ups to choose from: 3 product directions, 5 teardown styles, 6 intro openings
  • When I point out one problem, first ask whether it's a site-wide rule; if so, write the rule, then fix every place at once
  • Change only what was pointed out; leave everything else alone
  • "Fixed" needs evidence: screenshots, measured numbers, side by side with photos of the real thing
  • Mistakes get stated plainly: what went wrong, for how long, and whether it's fixed

Calls I made

  • Four Taiwanese vehicles with completely different shapes to prove the framework, instead of chasing numbers
  • One layer at a time: fewer names in exchange for something kids understand
  • Accuracy needs sources, and inferred parts are written down
  • Zhuyin is the foundation of the product, not an option
  • Performance is part of the experience: smooth first, then beautiful
What's next

What's next

  • Measure load and frame times on older iPads and mid-range Android phones, and set the minimum supported device
  • Sit beside kids while they play and note how deep they peel, which parts they tap and where they get stuck
  • Add a fifth vehicle that changes shape (an excavator or a garbage truck) to see whether the framework holds
  • Build a quiz: after a layer, say a name and ask the kid to tap it
  • Replace the Model Y body with a commercially licensed version before deciding whether to charge
