Case study · OneStroke

A tennis product that explains
every stroke from a phone video

I practise on my own at an indoor ball-machine court. The analysis tools on the market gave me scores, but couldn't tell me what was off or what to practise next. I turned that frustration into a product ready to sell: upload a practice video and it cuts out every stroke, scores it, diagnoses it like a coach and rebuilds the player in 3D.

Problem

Plenty of scores, but no idea what to practise next

Where today's tools fall short

  • I ran the same video through OptiSwing: “arm rotation” came back as a flat 100. The score saturates and stops telling strokes apart
  • It gives a number for each item, but not which one matters most
  • There are no drills, and nothing checks next time whether you actually improved

What the alternatives cost

  • A private lesson costs about NT$800–1,600 an hour (PRO360 rates), and coaches rarely explain with data
  • Human video analysis from overseas costs £35–55 a session and takes days
  • The opening: everyone films on their phone, and pose-estimation models now run on an ordinary computer
Users & context

Serve one very specific situation first

The first version targets only my own situation, so every assumption can be checked against real data. The architecture leaves room for coaches with students.

Main user: amateurs practising on their own

Indoor ball-machine court, playing alone, phone fixed at the side of the court. About 10 minutes and 100+ strokes a session. They want to know what went well today, what to fix and what to practise next time.

Next user: coaches with students

They need each student's data kept apart, a link that shows students only their own sessions, and a formal report to hand over. This came after the core flow was validated, as the groundwork for accounts later.

Principles

Five rules I wrote down first

These rules sit at the top of the project spec, and every later trade-off was checked against them.

Licences first

To keep commercial use possible, every model choice favours commercially usable licences. The mainstream SMPL family of 3D body models can't be used commercially, so it was ruled out. Every model that comes in gets its version, licence and commercial status written down.

Raw values are the data; scores are a layer on top

Only raw measures are stored: angles, speeds, displacements. Scores are calculated when a report is made, and the scoring formula carries a version number. When the formula changes, every past session can be recalculated and the trend stays unbroken.

Every number has a source

Scoring references come from the sports biomechanics literature: the elite average maps to 80, and the average minus two standard deviations maps to 50. Each source is listed in the scoring notes.

Compare rankings, not absolute values

3D from a single camera is systematically off compared with lab motion capture. Validation doesn't ask for the same numbers, only the same ranking and trend: which strokes come out high and which come out low.

Talk like a coach

The reader is a player. The text uses plain words and body comparisons, such as “one ball in three” or “a fist's width”. The raw numbers sit in an expandable “See the data” section as evidence.

Decision log

Seven key trade-offs

Decision 01

Which model for the 3D body

Context
Measuring shoulder turn and arm rotation needs more than 2D, but the most accurate models are all SMPL-based, and their licences rule out commercial use.
Options
WHAM / GVHMR (SMPL)SAM 3D Body2D only
Decision
Meta's SAM 3D Body for 3D; Apache-2.0 RTMPose and MediaPipe for 2D. The clauses that still need a lawyer before commercial use are listed.
Result
The commercial path stays open, and the arm-rotation and wrist measures that 2D can't capture come with it.
Decision 02

What to do with measures 2D gets wrong

Context
A data audit comparing two pose models found that hip–shoulder separation didn't agree between them in 2D.
Decision
Take it out of the score and keep it as reference only. From then on, every measure is labelled reliable, reference or unreliable for each camera angle.
Result
Better one score fewer than a wrong number. When front and back angles were added later, this reliability table carried straight over.
Decision 03

3D is slow: move to the cloud?

Context
SAM 3D takes 13–25 s per stroke on a Mac, so a 150-stroke session needs more than 40 minutes. Cloud GPU costs hadn't been measured yet.
Options
All in the cloudAll localKey frames + interpolation, key strokes only
Decision
Compute key frames only and interpolate. After upload, 5 strokes are picked automatically (the session's best, a typical one, the plan's target and problem strokes, one to improve); the rest run on demand, and every run's usage is logged.
Result
3D per session dropped from over 40 minutes to about 2. The usage log became the billing basis for a “5 strokes included, full session extra” plan.
Decision 04

Without 3D, the skeleton often swaps front and back limbs

Context
2D only sees a flat image; depending on the angle, limbs were flipped front-to-back in 11–28% of frames.
Decision
Use SAM 3D results as the teacher to train a small model that reads only 2D and judges front and back, then blend it into the skeleton. I also confirmed the SAM licence doesn't forbid training on its outputs.
Result
Flips fell to 2% from the front, 3% from back-right, 9% from the side and 14% from behind, with no extra 3D compute.
Decision 05

What the share video should look like

Context
Players want to share their good shots, and that's the cheapest way to win new users. The brief: premium, shareable, showing off the impressive parts.
Decision
The default style is “3D bullet time”: the clip plays once → freezes at contact → the player becomes a 3D sculpture and orbits once → back to the clip for the follow-through. The closing brand frame is fixed and can't be turned off.
Result
Every share video is a product demo. The later “best vs typical” video reuses the same 3D stage.
Decision 06

Compress on the phone before uploading?

Context
One session's original footage is about 580 MB, and uploading from a phone is slow.
Experiment
Cutting clips on the phone first still meant uploading 79–84% of the data, so it wasn't worth it. Compressing to 720p at 2 Mbps cut the size to a quarter with almost the same stroke count and forehand/backhand split, but compression noise pushed speed measures up by 3–17%.
Decision
Not shipped yet. Inflated numbers mixed into the history would make players think they'd improved. It waits until there's a plan for joining old and new data.
Result
A simple-looking optimisation became a trade-off backed by data, protecting the core promise: trends you can trust.
Decision 07

How to split the data for students

Context
Getting ready for friends to try it, with accounts likely later.
Options
Tag the existing dataOne folder per player + a path layer
Decision
Go all the way: every path goes through one layer; each player gets their own settings, baseline, trends and home page; students get a personal link that sees and uploads only their own data; deleted items go to a recycle bin for 30 days.
Result
End-to-end tests passed (upload, 3D and the automatic analysis all land in the student's own folder). Moving to accounts later only changes the path layer.

Product

The product

Everything below is a real screen from my October 20 session (158 strokes). The long screenshots scroll inside the phone frames, and faces are blurred. The app's interface is in Chinese.

01

Home and plan: open it and know what to practise today

Home holds one thing: this week's focus and the latest session. The plan page turns the diagnosis into drills, sets and acceptance targets; the progress page shows score trends and how you compare with last time.

A session report: diagnosis → data → every stroke

First the one main problem and the strokes that prove it, then the full data, then a clip of every stroke. All three are full-page screenshots you can scroll inside the phone frame.

Every stroke can be viewed in 3D from any angle. One tap makes a vertical share video: bullet time, a swing light trail, and the best stroke orbiting next to a typical one.

After signing in, coaches see their own students first. Uploads are resumable, so a dropped connection picks up where it left off.

How I worked

How I split the work with AI

An AI coding agent wrote the code. My job was to build the right thing: define the problem, set acceptance criteria, choose between options and review every result. A few working rules kept it moving steadily and on course:

Rules

  • Work broken into steps that can each be accepted on their own: clips → 2D measures and scoring → 3D → analysis and trends
  • Before any screen feature ships, it's generated for real on every session and every camera angle (a rule that came from one bad delivery)
  • Visual decisions start as mock-ups for me to choose from, never as direct edits
  • When several sessions work on the same project at once, each commits only its own changes

Calls I made

  • Start with one concrete situation, then widen step by step (ball-machine court → any angle → students)
  • Turn down optimisations that look quick when they would hurt trends you can trust
  • Design the share video as a way to win users, not as an add-on
  • Get the data structure right for accounts before building them
What's next

Next steps

  • Recalibrate speed measures, arm rotation and wrist flick with 60 fps video
  • Run elite players' reference videos through the same pipeline to correct single-camera bias (scoring v2 calibration)
  • Measure cloud GPU cost and speed, then add accounts and payments
  • Widen the trial to players of different levels, handedness and camera angles
